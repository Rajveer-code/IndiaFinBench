\section{Scoring Pipeline Validation}
\label{app:judge}

The automated scoring pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:scoring}) uses four stages: exact match, fuzzy token matching (RapidFuzz \texttt{token\_set\_ratio} $\geq$ 0.72), numerical extraction match, and Yes/No match for contradiction detection. While rigorous, the pipeline penalizes answers that are semantically correct but formatted differently from the reference answer---a systematic limitation of string-based matching for open-ended regulatory QA.

\subsection{Judge Rubric}

Both judges used in this paper---the Gemini 2.5 Flash pilot described below and the phi4-mini cross-model judge of Section~\ref{sec:judge}---were given the identical rubric, at temperature 0:

\begin{quote}\small\noindent
Determine if the model prediction is CORRECT.\\
Rules:\\
--- CORRECT if the final value or fact matches, even if: different formatting (currency symbols, comma separators, units), extra calculation steps shown before the final answer, paraphrasing of the same regulatory rule, or rounding within 1\%.\\
--- INCORRECT only if: wrong number, wrong threshold, wrong date, wrong entity, or contradicts the reference.\\
Reply with exactly: CORRECT or INCORRECT, plus a one-sentence reason.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Gemini Pilot}

Before committing to a full-coverage second judge, we piloted semantic re-scoring with Gemini 2.5 Flash---one of the twelve evaluated models---over items strict scoring marked incorrect across NUM, REG, and TMP. The strict pipeline flagged 935 such predictions; 874 were judged, and the remaining 61 (58 empty responses, 3 API failures) were retained as incorrect without review, since no semantic judgment can rescue an empty prediction. CON items use exact Yes/No matching and were not part of this pilot.

Judge reliability was estimated on a stratified 28-item manual audit (20 NUM, 5 REG, 3 TMP). Manual review confirmed 25 of 28 verdicts (89.3\%) as correct. The three judge errors all involved multi-step numerical problems where the model showed correct intermediate reasoning but reached a wrong final answer (NUM\_074: predicted Rs.~19,000 crore vs.\ reference Rs.~33,000 crore; NUM\_103: concluded wrong direction for a threshold comparison; NUM\_136: applied wrong regulatory fee slab)---the judge's tendency to over-credit partial reasoning chains on NUM items, referenced in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Across the 874 judged predictions, Gemini reclassified 685 from incorrect to correct, an overall flip rate of 78.4\% (REG: 261/327 = 79.8\%; NUM: 272/348 = 78.2\%; TMP: 152/199 = 76.4\%).

Because Gemini is itself one of the evaluated models and this pilot covered only strict failures---so it could characterise strict-to-judge overturns but not the judge's rejection rate of strict-correct predictions---these results motivated, rather than substitute for, the full-coverage cross-model judge (phi4-mini, sharing no model family with any evaluated system, reviewing all 4{,}128 REG/NUM/TMP predictions including strict-correct ones) whose results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:judge} and Table~\ref{tab:judgereliability}. Item-level Gemini verdicts remain available at \texttt{evaluation/results\_judged/} in the repository; the judge-only and judge-augmented accuracies used throughout this paper are computed from phi4-mini's full-coverage pass (\texttt{evaluation/results\_judged\_phi4/}), not from this pilot.

\subsection{Root Causes}

Reading a sample of overturned items during the Gemini pilot surfaces three recurring surface-form patterns. This is a qualitative description of what these items look like, not a quantified account of what predicts reclassification in general---Section~\ref{sec:regime} tests output length directly and finds no association that survives adjustment for task and model composition, so none of the three should be read as an established mechanism. (1) \textit{Numeric format differences} --- models frequently use digits (`50\%', `365 days') while references use written numbers (`fifty per cent', `three hundred and sixty-five days'). (2) \textit{Explanatory prefixes} --- models prepend calculation steps or regulatory citations before stating the final value. (3) \textit{Abbreviated answers} --- models give a shorter but correct version of the reference (e.g., ref = `Six months after completion of the open offer'; pred = `Six months').

\subsection{Logging Truncation}
\label{app:truncation}

Predictions are character-capped at write time by the evaluation harness, at a limit that varies by
which script produced the row (200, 300, or 500 characters). 554 of the 4{,}128 judged REG/NUM/TMP
rows (13.4\%) sit exactly at their model's cap, ranging from 31.1\% (Gemini 2.5 Flash, 200-char cap)
to 0.3\% (LLaMA-3-8B and Mistral-7B, both repaired to a higher effective cap during the truncation
fix described below). The judge previously imposed a second, undisclosed layer of truncation on its
own inputs (question capped at 600 characters, reference at 300, prediction at 500), independent of
the logging cap above; this has been removed. \texttt{scripts/judge\_phi4\_crossmodel.py} now sizes
Ollama's context window (\texttt{num\_ctx}) per run from the longest question+reference+prediction
triple in the corpus being judged, with a 25\% safety margin over the estimated token count plus the
150-token output budget---2{,}048 tokens for the released judged data, logged in
\texttt{evaluation/judge\_run\_manifest.json} together with an assertion, checked per item, that every
prompt fits; zero of the 4{,}128 judged rows were flagged as not fitting. The 449 rows whose reference
answer or prediction had been cut by the old 300/500-character judge-input caps (concentrated on
Qwen3-32B, Llama 4 Scout 17B, LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B, and Gemini 2.5 Pro's judged files) were
re-judged under the fixed pipeline: 52 verdicts changed (19 strict-wrong items newly judged correct,
33 newly judged incorrect), a mixed-direction shift consistent with removing a text-availability
artifact rather than an infrastructure failure. No response includes a provider-reported finish-reason,
stop-reason, or token-usage field anywhere in the released data, so generation-level truncation---as
opposed to the logging truncation quantified here---cannot be measured from the original release at
all; the matched-budget re-run (Section~\ref{sec:budget}) closes this gap directly, with full,
untruncated responses and finish-reason logging for every model reached.

Restricting to the 190 of 344 REG/NUM/TMP items where every one of the twelve models' predictions is
strictly below its own cap---so no model's prediction on that item could possibly be a truncation
artifact---DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B remains last of twelve under strict scoring (70.0\%) and
reaches the top tier under the judge-augmented sensitivity regime (98.4\%, 5-way tie for the top
rank). The qualitative pattern this paper reports is not an artifact of logging truncation, though
the exact ranks and margins on the truncation-free subset differ modestly from the full-sample
figures, as expected with a smaller item count.

\subsection{Human Adjudication Protocol}

To check both judges against a human reader, we built a 238-item adjudication sheet (\texttt{annotation/judge\_audit/adjudication\_sheet.csv}, \texttt{scripts/build\_adjudication\_control\_sample.py}): the 174 REG/NUM/TMP items where Gemini and phi4-mini disagree---the only cases informative about which judge is right---plus a stratified control sample of 64 items (8 per stratum, fixed random seed) spanning cases where the available methods \emph{agree}: \texttt{\{REG,NUM,TMP\}\_strict\_incorrect\_judges\_agree} (both judges concur an item is wrong), \texttt{\{REG,NUM,TMP\}\_strict\_correct\_phi4\_agrees} (phi4-mini does not dispute a strict-correct item; no Gemini verdict exists for these, since Gemini only ever judged strict failures), and two CON strata (\texttt{CON\_strict\_correct\_no\_judge}, \texttt{CON\_strict\_incorrect\_no\_judge}) that spot-check the exact-match scorer directly, since CON is never judge-reviewed by either model. This sample is evidence about specific, informative cases, not a random draw sized to estimate a population-level judge error rate, and Section~\ref{sec:judge} reports it as such.

\subsection{Human Adjudication Results}

All 238 items were adjudicated by the author, scored against the judge verdicts as they stood at the time of adjudication (Section~\ref{sec:judge}). Section~\ref{sec:judge} reports the headline split (89.1\% control-agreement, 56.9\%/43.1\% Gemini/phi4-mini on disputed items); per stratum, the control-agreement rate ranged from 75.0\% (\texttt{NUM\_strict\_incorrect\_judges\_agree}, \texttt{TMP\_strict\_correct\_phi4\_agrees}) to 100\% (both CON strata and \texttt{REG\_strict\_correct\_phi4\_agrees}), with no stratum below 75\%---agreement between methods tracked human judgment reasonably well across every category sampled, not only on average. On the 174 disagreement items the human/Gemini--human/phi4-mini split is widest on numerical reasoning (Gemini matched 44 of 68, 64.7\%, against phi4-mini's 24, 35.3\%) and narrows to near-even on regulatory interpretation (30 of 59 vs.\ 29 of 59) and temporal reasoning (25 of 47 vs.\ 22 of 47). Section~\ref{sec:limitations} reports the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-specific breakdown of the disputed-item sample.

\subsection{Matched-Budget Re-run: Provider and Model Identifiers}
\label{app:matched-budget-ids}

Four of the original Groq-hosted models (LLaMA-3.3-70B, Llama 4 Scout 17B, Qwen3-32B, Kimi K2) were
confirmed removed from Groq's own model catalogue as of 2026-09-03 (a live \texttt{models.list()}
call against Groq returns only the two GPT-OSS models from the original six); their open-weight
checkpoints were re-run via OpenRouter instead, under exact identifiers verified live against
OpenRouter's models endpoint: \texttt{meta-llama/llama-3.3-70b-instruct} (Meta: Llama 3.3 70B
Instruct), \texttt{meta-llama/llama-4-scout} (Meta: Llama 4 Scout), \texttt{qwen/qwen3-32b} (Qwen:
Qwen3 32B), \texttt{moonshotai/kimi-k2} (MoonshotAI: Kimi K2, the \texttt{0711} dated snapshot ---
the original Groq-served checkpoint's exact build date is not recoverable, since Groq no longer
serves it to check against). Qwen3-32B's OpenRouter route (Nebius) answers in extended-thinking mode
by default, consuming budget on invisible reasoning before any visible answer; every Qwen3-32B call
in the matched-budget re-run appends the model's own documented \texttt{/no\_think} directive to
suppress this, matching the direct-answer, no-preamble system prompt used throughout. DeepSeek-R1-
Distill-Llama-70B was already OpenRouter-hosted in the original run and uses the same identifier,
\texttt{deepseek/deepseek-r1-distill-llama-70b}, unchanged. Gemini 2.5 Pro is excluded from the
matched-budget re-run: a live call against \texttt{gemini-2.5-pro} on a fresh API key returns
\texttt{404 NOT\_FOUND}, ``This model \ldots{} is no longer available to new users'' --- a platform
access restriction, not a credentials problem, and not something a different model generation (e.g.
\texttt{gemini-3.1-pro-preview}) can substitute for without re-running a different model than the
one this paper reports on.

Gemini 2.5 Flash's direct Google AI Studio route throttled after roughly 20 requests regardless of
request spacing (confirmed at 8s and 15s spacing, via the exact
\texttt{generate\_content\_free\_tier\_requests} quota-exceeded message, well below the model's
documented rate limit, consistent with a new-project ramp-up restriction), and did not clear across
several hours of intermittent checking. We re-ran it instead through OpenRouter's routing to the
same model (\texttt{google/gemini-2.5-flash}), with the request explicitly restricted to Google AI
Studio as the serving provider (\texttt{provider.only=["google-ai-studio"]}) at its discounted
Flex service tier (\$0.15/M prompt, \$1.25/M completion tokens, verified live against OpenRouter's
own endpoint-pricing listing before running), a price cap set to prevent any silent fallback to a
costlier tier, and \texttt{reasoning.max\_tokens=0} to disable Gemini's hidden-thinking mode (the
same failure class that required Qwen3-32B's \texttt{/no\_think} directive above). A mandatory cost
probe (8 items, then a 12-item sample stratified across all four task types) confirmed zero
reasoning tokens on every item and projected a full-406-item cost of roughly \$0.02 before the full
run was authorized. All 406 items completed with zero errors at a total cost of \$0.0243, confirmed
served entirely by Google AI Studio at the Flex tier throughout
(\texttt{evaluation/gemini\_flash\_openrouter\_manifest.json}). This is a transport change, not a
model substitution: the same Gemini 2.5 Flash weights, reached through a different request path
after the direct route proved unusable within a practical time budget.

\subsection{Budget Selection and the DeepSeek-R1-Distill Outlier}
\label{app:budget-outlier}

The matched budget (512 tokens) was chosen from a pilot across three candidates (512, 1024, 2048) on
a stratified 24-item subset (8 each of REG/NUM/TMP) across all reachable models
(\texttt{scripts/matched\_budget\_pilot.py}): mean truncation across models was 1.1\% at 512, 0.4\% at
1024, and 0.0\% at 2048---raising the budget past 512 buys a reduction of about one point in an
already-negligible rate, so 512 was taken as the lowest budget that does not materially change
truncation relative to the higher candidates.

DeepSeek-R1-Distill's $+10.59$pp delta (Table~\ref{tab:matchedbudget}) is the one result in this
re-run we could not fully explain and report as such rather than resolving it into a clean story. Two
candidate contributors are on the table, neither ruled in or out. First, logging truncation:
DeepSeek's original release had 19.5\% of its REG/NUM/TMP rows sitting at the old 300-character
write-time cap (Appendix~\ref{app:truncation}), the highest rate of any model with a comparably small
delta, so removing that cap plausibly recovers some of the swing---but a Pearson correlation between
each model's original at-cap rate and its matched-budget delta, computed across the other ten
models (\texttt{scripts/matched\_budget\_correlation.py}), is $r = -0.77$: models with a
\emph{higher} original truncation rate tend to show a \emph{smaller or negative} delta, the opposite
of what a truncation-driven recovery story predicts, and the single most truncated model in the
original release (Gemini 2.5 Flash, 31.1\%) shows the most negative delta of the ten ($-3.70$pp).
The two largest positive deltas among the ten (LLaMA-3-8B, $+4.19$pp; Mistral-7B, $+1.97$pp) belong
to the only two models run locally via Ollama, with unchanged serving between the original run and
the re-run and near-zero original truncation (0.3\% each); every remaining, transport-changed
model---whether it stayed on its original provider (GPT-OSS 120B/20B, Gemma 3 4B), moved to
OpenRouter for the re-run (LLaMA-3.3-70B, Llama 4 Scout 17B, Qwen3-32B, Kimi K2), or moved from
direct access to OpenRouter-routed access to the same provider (Gemini 2.5 Flash)---shows a flat or
negative delta regardless of its original truncation rate. This pattern argues against
logging-truncation removal as the dominant driver of the matched-budget deltas generally, and toward
the second candidate below, though neither explains on its own why DeepSeek specifically moved as
far as it did. Second, output-style or serving variance: a direct probe of the same OpenRouter
endpoint (\texttt{deepseek/deepseek-r1-distill-llama-70b}) made while preparing this analysis
returned \texttt{provider: Novita} with the response's \texttt{content} field empty and a
\texttt{reasoning} field populated instead---the same budget-consumed-by-hidden-reasoning failure
mode Qwen3-32B needed an explicit \texttt{/no\_think} directive to avoid (Section~\ref{app:matched-budget-ids}
above). Manual inspection of matched-run predictions confirms a stylistic shift on several items from
the original's more discursive phrasing (e.g. ``The minimum average operating profit required is
fifteen crore rupees over the preceding three years. \textbackslash n\textbackslash nAnswer: Fifteen
crore rupees.'') to a terser, more exact-match-friendly form (``fifteen crore rupees''), which is
consistent with either explanation and does not distinguish between them. Open-weight checkpoints
served through a third-party aggregator are not guaranteed stable in underlying serving behaviour
between the original evaluation and a re-run weeks later, and we cannot rule out that DeepSeek-R1-
Distill's serving changed in that window independent of the budget we varied. We report the number,
the two candidate explanations, and the evidence against attributing it cleanly to either, rather than
picking the more convenient story.

\paragraph{Representative failure examples.}

\textbf{Domain Knowledge Failure (Gemma 3 4B, Temporal Reasoning, item TMP\_039).} Asked whether a December~15, 2026 OTC derivative transaction is subject to Unique Transaction Identifier requirements that take effect January~1, 2027, the model answers ``Yes,'' misapplying the forward-looking effective date to a transaction entered into before it; the reference answer is ``No,'' since the requirements do not yet apply. (Hard temporal-reasoning items map to DKF under the taxonomy of Appendix~\ref{sec:errortax-sub}, since the failure is a substantive misunderstanding of when a rule takes effect, not a surface mismatch.)

\textbf{Numerical Reasoning Failure (GPT-OSS 120B, Numerical Reasoning).} Given an RBI calculation requiring the maximum eligible dividend as a percentage of adjusted PAT, the model correctly identifies the relevant table but applies the wrong conditional threshold, computing the dividend at a higher rate than is warranted by the given capital ratio.

\textbf{Temporal Reasoning Failure (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B, Temporal Reasoning, item TMP\_036).} Asked when the SEBI (Research Analysts) Regulations 2014---published September~1, 2014, effective ninety days later---came into force, the model answers ``November 29, 2014''; the reference date is November 30, 2014, one day later. The model's day-count method is correct; its arithmetic is off by one day.

\textbf{Context Grounding Failure (LLaMA-3.3-70B, Contradiction Detection).} Given two RBI passages specifying the same 5\% non-competitive bidding allocation limit in different phrasing (`five per cent' vs.\ `5\%'), the model incorrectly identifies a contradiction based on surface-level differences rather than recognizing semantic equivalence.

\section{Few-Shot Evaluation}
\label{app:fewshot}

To assess whether zero-shot evaluation represents a conservative lower bound on model capability, we evaluated four top-performing models---Gemini 2.5 Flash, LLaMA-3.3-70B, Llama 4 Scout 17B, and Qwen3-32B---under 3-shot prompting. For each task type, three fixed in-context examples were prepended to the prompt, drawn from the same distribution as the benchmark items and held constant across all models. All other evaluation conditions (system prompt, scoring pipeline, dataset) were identical to the zero-shot setup.

3-shot prompting improves numerical reasoning by 2.1--16.3 percentage points across all four models, with Llama 4 Scout 17B showing the largest NUM gain (+16.3 pp, from 66.3\% to 82.6\%). LLaMA-3.3-70B gains +7.7 pp on temporal reasoning under 3-shot. These improvements are consistent with the direction of the zero-shot conservatism claimed in Section~\ref{sec:main-results-text}; with four models and one prompting configuration, this is suggestive rather than a controlled test of that claim.

CON performance is mixed: Llama 4 Scout 17B declines 12.9 pp (from near-ceiling 98.4\% to 85.5\%), while Gemini 2.5 Flash improves +3.2 pp. Performance degradation under 3-shot on tasks with near-ceiling zero-shot accuracy is consistent with in-context example interference, where the few-shot prefix biases the model away from clear Yes/No responses on straightforward contradiction items. Qwen3-32B is unaffected on CON and TMP (0.0 pp), suggesting its zero-shot behavior is already well-calibrated for these task types.

Overall, the broad performance tier ordering is preserved under 3-shot prompting (Gemini 2.5 Flash $\geq$ Qwen3-32B $\geq$ LLaMA-3.3-70B $\approx$ Llama 4 Scout 17B). We do not read this as establishing that the tier structure reflects genuine capability differences rather than prompting sensitivity: the same four models show a 12.9-point CON decline for Llama 4 Scout 17B under the identical 3-shot condition, which is itself a prompting-sensitivity effect. The evidence supports only the narrower claim that overall ordering is not obviously disrupted by this one alternative prompting configuration.
