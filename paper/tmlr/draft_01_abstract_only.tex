\begin{abstract}
A benchmark leaderboard reports one accuracy number, but that number is produced by a scoring rule
as much as by a model. We ask what changes when only the rule changes. IndiaFinBench, introduced
here, is a 406-item expert-annotated benchmark over SEBI and RBI regulatory text covering
regulatory interpretation, numerical reasoning, contradiction detection, and temporal reasoning; it
is built on jurisdiction-specific regulatory text that is less represented in common Western
financial benchmarks, reducing the likelihood that performance is explained by benchmark-specific
familiarity. We evaluate twelve contemporary LLMs zero-shot under two scoring regimes: a
strict four-stage string-matching pipeline, and a judge-audited regime in which a cross-model
judge---sharing no model family with any evaluated system---reviews every regulatory-interpretation,
numerical-reasoning, and temporal-reasoning prediction, not only the ones strict scoring marks wrong.

The regimes disagree, and the disagreement is not cosmetic. Strict accuracy spans 75.1--89.7\%;
judge-audited accuracy spans 94.1--98.0\%. DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B, a reasoning-distilled model, ranks
12th of twelve (last place) under strict scoring and reaches an exact tie for 1st under judge-audited
scoring (both DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B and LLaMA-3.3-70B at 398/406, 98.0\%), because
93.9\% of its strict errors are reclassified as correct by the cross-model judge, primarily
reflecting format differences. Eleven of the
twelve models move by two or more ranks, and the two orderings are essentially uncorrelated (Spearman
$\rho = 0.067$,
$p = 0.84$); in this 12-model sample, the strict and judge-audited rankings show weak correspondence,
illustrating how ranking can depend strongly on the scoring regime. Judge auditing
also compresses the field: the twelve-model spread narrows from 14.5 points under strict scoring to
3.9 under judge-audited scoring. A substantial portion of the observed spread is attributable to
format-sensitive scoring rather than reasoning differences, under our judge-audited analysis.

Item-level analysis compounds the problem. At least 11 of the 12 models answer 213 of the 406 items
correctly, so those items separate almost no pair of systems; the benchmark's discriminative signal
rests on 134 items. Nominal size and effective size are not the same quantity, and benchmarks
rarely report the difference.

We also anchor the scale against a person. On a 60-item subset drawn from the medium and hard
strata, one non-specialist evaluator scored 80.0\% (48/60; 95\% Wilson CI [68.2, 88.2]). This is a
single evaluator and a reference point, not a population estimate: paired bootstrap tests against
all twelve models find no significant difference in either direction once corrected for the
twelve-test family. Dataset, evaluation harness, per-item judge verdicts,
and all model predictions are released.
\end{abstract}
