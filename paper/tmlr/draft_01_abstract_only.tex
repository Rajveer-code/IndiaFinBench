\begin{abstract}
A benchmark leaderboard reports one accuracy number, but that number is a joint product of a model
and a scoring rule. We ask what changes when only the rule changes, and find that the ordering of
evaluated systems is highly sensitive to it. IndiaFinBench, introduced here, is a 406-item
expert-annotated benchmark over SEBI and RBI regulatory text covering regulatory interpretation,
numerical reasoning, contradiction detection, and temporal reasoning; it is built on
jurisdiction-specific regulatory text that is less represented in common Western financial
benchmarks, reducing the likelihood that performance is explained by benchmark-specific familiarity.
We score twelve contemporary LLMs, evaluated zero-shot, under two primary scoring regimes over the
same predictions---strict four-stage string matching, and the verdicts of a cross-model judge
sharing no model family with any evaluated system---plus one operational composite regime
(strict-or-judge, included to quantify asymmetric judge application rather than proposed as
authoritative).

The two primary regimes rank the field in mildly opposite directions: Spearman $\rho = -0.224$
between strict and judge-only accuracy across the twelve models ($p = 0.48$), and no model holds
its rank across all three regimes. Strict accuracy spans 75.12--89.66\% (Gemini~2.5~Flash highest,
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B lowest); judge-only accuracy spans 87.19--95.32\% with a different
leader and trailer (Llama~4~Scout~17B highest, Gemma~3~4B lowest); the composite regime compresses
the field further, to 94.1--98.0\%, under which DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B and LLaMA-3.3-70B tie
for first despite DeepSeek ranking last under strict scoring, because the judge reclassifies 93.9\%
of its strict errors as correct. It is tempting to attribute reclassification to output verbosity;
we test that directly rather than assume it, and it does not survive adjustment for task and model
composition, a negative result we report as such.

Item-level analysis compounds the finding. At least 11 of the 12 models answer 213 of the 406 items
correctly, so those items separate almost no pair of systems; the benchmark's discriminative
coverage rests on 134 items. Nominal size and discriminative coverage are not the same quantity, and
benchmarks rarely report the difference.

We also anchor the scale against a person. On a 60-item subset drawn from the medium and hard
strata, one non-specialist evaluator scored 80.0\% (48/60; 95\% Wilson CI [68.2, 88.2]). This is a
single evaluator and a reference point, not a population estimate: paired bootstrap tests against
all twelve models find no significant difference in either direction once corrected for the
twelve-test family. Dataset, evaluation harness, per-item judge verdicts,
and all model predictions are released.
\end{abstract}
