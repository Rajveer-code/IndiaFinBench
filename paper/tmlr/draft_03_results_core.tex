\section{The Scoring Regime Reorders the Leaderboard}
\label{sec:regime}

Every model was scored twice over identical predictions. The strict regime is the four-stage
string-matching pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:scoring}. The corrected regime takes every prediction
the strict pipeline marked wrong on REG, NUM, and TMP---874 items across the twelve models---and
submits it to an item-level judge that sees the question, the reference answer, and the prediction,
and returns a verdict with a one-sentence rationale. Contradiction-detection items use exact Yes/No
matching and need no semantic review. The judge reclassified 685 of the 874 flagged predictions as
correct, an overall flip rate of 78.4\% (REG 261/327, NUM 272/348, TMP 152/199). A stratified
28-item manual audit of the judge's own verdicts confirmed 25 of 28, or 89.3\%.

Nothing about the models changed between the two regimes. Only the rule changed.

The regimes disagree about who is best. Strict accuracy spans 70.4--89.7\%, corrected accuracy
79.8--96.6\%, and the two orderings are only weakly related: Spearman $\rho = 0.413$ ($p = 0.18$),
Kendall $\tau = 0.313$ ($p = 0.17$). With twelve models these tests have little power, so we do not
claim the rankings are independent---we claim the strict ranking is a poor predictor of the
corrected one, which Figure~\ref{fig:regime} and Table~\ref{tab:regime} show directly. Eight of the
twelve models move by two or more positions.

A second effect is visible in the accuracies rather than the ranks. Setting aside Gemma 4 E4B, the
weakest model and the only one whose errors survive the audit in bulk, the remaining eleven models
span 14.6 points under strict scoring (75.1--89.7) and 6.0 points under corrected scoring
(90.6--96.6). Correcting for format compliance compresses the field to a little over a third of its
apparent spread, and three models tie outright at 95.6. Most of what a strict leaderboard displays
as a capability gap between mid-table models is not a capability gap.

The clearest case is DeepSeek R1 70B, which ranks 11th of twelve under strict scoring and 1st under
corrected scoring. Of its 101 strict errors, 87 are reclassified as correct---86.1\%---leaving 14
genuine failures, the fewest of any model in the study. Its strict score is not a measurement of
its regulatory reasoning. It is a measurement of how far its output format sits from the reference
format, and the two happen to be far apart because the model narrates its working. The system
prompt asked for an answer with no preamble; a model whose signature behaviour is visible reasoning
is penalised for exactly that behaviour, under a rule that cannot tell narration from error.

This has a practical reading that is not a criticism of string matching. A compliance workflow that
consumes model output through a format-sensitive parser really would fail on those 87 items, and
for that deployment question the strict number is the honest one. What the strict number cannot do
is answer the capability question, and the two are routinely reported as if they were the same
quantity. A leaderboard that reports one accuracy figure has silently chosen which question to
answer, without saying so.

\section{Effective Size: How Many Items Actually Discriminate}
\label{sec:effsize}

A benchmark's nominal size is the number of items it contains. Its effective size, for the purpose
of separating systems, is the number of items on which systems actually differ. An item that every
model answers correctly contributes to reported accuracy but distinguishes no pair of models.

For each item we take $p$, the fraction of the twelve models answering it correctly, and summarise
its spread as $s = 4p(1-p)$, which is maximised at $p = 0.5$ and zero when every model agrees. This
is a descriptive statistic over our twelve-model panel, not a fitted item-response model: we make no
latent-ability assumptions and estimate no per-item slope parameters. It answers only the question
we are asking---how much does this item separate the systems we ran?

Half the benchmark sits at ceiling. 213 of 406 items are answered correctly by at least 11 of the
12 models, and 7 more are answered correctly by at most one, so 220 items---slightly over half---
carry almost no information about the relative ordering of these systems. The median item is
answered correctly by 91.7\% of models, and mean spread across all items is 0.419. By the criterion
in Table~\ref{tab:effsize}, 134 items do the discriminating.

An independent route gives the same count. Analysing consensus rather than spread, 213 items are
answered correctly by more than 90\% of models and 11 items by fewer than 20\%. The ceiling count
of 213 is reached by two calculations that share no threshold, which is the reassurance we can
offer that it is not an artifact of where the cut was placed.

We report this about our own benchmark deliberately. It bears directly on how the results above
should be read: a gap of a few points between two mid-table models rests on a subset of 134 items,
not 406, and confidence intervals computed over the full item count understate that. It also
suggests a construction lesson we did not anticipate when building the benchmark---annotating for
coverage of a domain and annotating for discrimination between systems are different objectives,
and expert effort spent on items that every competent model answers correctly buys descriptive
completeness rather than measurement power.

We do not claim this ratio is unusual. We claim it is unmeasured: we are not aware of comparable
benchmarks in this literature that report what fraction of their items separate the systems they
rank, and without that figure a reported accuracy gap cannot be sized against the evidence
supporting it.
