\subsection{Cross-Model Judge}
\label{sec:judge}

An LLM-as-judge audit is only as convincing as the judge's independence from what it is judging.
The item-level judge used to compute judge-audited accuracy is Gemini 2.5 Flash---one of the twelve
evaluated models. To assess whether this affects the audit, we additionally judge every REG/NUM/TMP
prediction with \textbf{phi4-mini} (Microsoft Phi, 3.8B parameters), run locally via Ollama at zero
cost. Phi shares no model family with any of the twelve benchmarked systems. We call this a
\textbf{cross-model} judge, not an independent or ground-truth one: phi4-mini is a small model, and
disagreement between it and Gemini 2.5 Flash is compatible with either judge being wrong, which is
why we pair it with human adjudication rather than treating its verdict as final.

The cross-model pass covers \textbf{every} REG/NUM/TMP prediction---344 items $\times$ 12 models
$=$ 4{,}128 judgements---not only the items strict scoring marks wrong. Full coverage is what makes
a \emph{judge rejection rate of strict-correct predictions} estimable for the first time: the
original audit design, by construction, could only characterise strict-to-judge overturns. CON is excluded from both judges, matching
the pipeline's existing rationale (Section~\ref{sec:scoring}): it is scored by exact Yes/No match
against an unambiguous binary label, which does not benefit from semantic review. Excluding it is
conservative for the disagreement rates we report below, not convenient: CON is the one task where
strict and judge-based scoring would agree almost by construction, so its absence removes a source
of trivial agreement rather than manufacturing disagreement elsewhere.

Before committing to the full pass we validated the judge on two small, targeted samples: phi4-mini
agreed with all eight items Gemini 2.5 Flash itself answered correctly under strict scoring (a
sanity check, not evidence of discrimination), and on eight DeepSeek-R1-Distill items marked
incorrect under strict scoring, flipped seven to correct---each a genuine format mismatch---while
correctly holding the eighth, where the predicted figure simply did not match the reference. The
pilot showed that phi4-mini both overturned strict failures and retained cases where the substantive
answer remained incorrect.

Table~\ref{tab:judgereliability} reports, for every model, the strict-to-judge overturn rate (items
strict scoring marked wrong that phi4-mini marks correct), the judge rejection rate of strict-correct
predictions (the reverse---estimable for the first time because this pass covers every item, not
only strict failures), and the resulting 406-item judge-audited accuracy. We avoid ``false
negative''/``false positive'' here: those terms presuppose the judge is ground truth, which it is
not (Section~\ref{sec:judge} reports its own error rate against human adjudication). On the 874 items Gemini 2.5 Flash
originally judged, phi4-mini agrees with it on 703 (\textbf{80.4\% raw agreement}). This is neither
suspiciously high (which would suggest one judge merely rubber-stamping the other) nor low enough to
call either judge unreliable outright; it indicates real, bounded disagreement between two models
neither of which we treat as ground truth. To resolve genuine disagreement between judges and check whether agreement between methods also
tracks human judgment, we hand-adjudicate 238 items: the 174 REG/NUM/TMP items where Gemini and
phi4-mini disagree, plus a stratified 64-item control sample where they agree (Appendix~\ref{app:judge}
reports the full sampling protocol). Adjudication is scored against the judge verdicts as they stood
when the sample was drawn and handed to the adjudicator---not a later-updated state---so every
disagreement item resolves to exactly one judge by construction; a subsequent data-quality fix
(Section~\ref{sec:models}) that later corrected truncated reference text for a few items is reported
independently rather than folded into this analysis. On the 64 control items the adjudicator
concurred with the existing consensus 89.1\% of the time. On the 174 disagreement items, the human's
verdict matched Gemini's on 56.9\% and phi4-mini's on 43.1\%, widest on numerical reasoning
(Appendix~\ref{app:judge} reports the full per-task breakdown). Neither judge is right often enough
on these disputed cases to be treated as ground truth, and phi4-mini's lower share here does not
argue against using it as this paper's primary judge: that choice rests on full coverage
(Section~\ref{sec:judge} could estimate false positives for the first time only because of it), not
on a claim that phi4-mini is more accurate than Gemini. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} returns to this
as a limitation of the cross-model judge design.

\textbf{Decision rule, stated before the results were examined.} If the cross-model judge supports
DeepSeek-R1-Distill's strict-12th-to-judge-audited-1st reversal (Section~\ref{sec:regime}), we report it
as the paper's central finding. If it does not, we report the discrepancy plainly and the paper's
central finding becomes the effective-size result (Section~\ref{sec:effsize}) instead. We commit to
this rule here, before running the full pass, specifically so that the choice of headline result is
not made after seeing which one looks better.

\textbf{What survives the judge audit.} Classifying every strict failure into four error types
(domain-knowledge, numerical, temporal, or context-grounding; Appendix~\ref{sec:errortax-sub}) shows
the reclassification is uneven, not uniform. Temporal-supersession failures dominate every profiled
model's residual, judge-retained errors (50--62\%), while numerical and domain-knowledge format
mismatches are cleared consistently. DeepSeek-R1-Distill's 101 strict failures collapse to 8
residual---the largest correction of any model and the basis for its rank reversal---while Gemma
3~4B, despite a comparable 72.1\% reclassification rate, keeps a residual error rate 1.7--3$\times$
every other profiled model's. Appendix~\ref{sec:errortax-sub} reports the full per-model breakdown
and representative failure examples.

\subsection{Statistical Significance and Performance Tiers}
\label{sec:significance}

Paired bootstrap significance testing (10,000 resamples) across all 66 model pairs finds 36 pairs
significantly different at $p < 0.05$, of which 15 survive Bonferroni correction
($\alpha = 0.05/66 \approx 0.00076$). Three descriptive performance clusters organize the results
under strict scoring; their boundaries are robust at the top and bottom of the ranking but
statistical, not categorical, in the middle.

\textbf{Tier 1 --- strong performers (81.5--89.7\%):} Gemini 2.5 Flash, Qwen3-32B, LLaMA-3.3-70B,
Llama 4 Scout 17B, and Kimi K2, all four of the latter statistically indistinguishable from one
another ($p$ = 0.06--0.79), forming a genuine plateau rather than a fine-grained ranking.
\textbf{The remaining seven models (70--79\%)} do not separate into a clean second and third tier:
Gemma 3 4B (78.8\%) is significantly below only the five Tier 1 models and is statistically
indistinguishable from every other model in the field ($p$ = 0.21--0.73), including
DeepSeek-R1-Distill, Mistral-7B, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and both GPT-OSS variants. Under strict scoring the
bottom seven models form one broad, largely undifferentiated cluster rather than the two-tier
structure a cursory read of the ranking would suggest.

Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to two comparisons inside the Tier 1 plateau---Llama 4 Scout
17B vs.\ LLaMA-3.3-70B and GPT-OSS 120B vs.\ 20B---where a large parameter difference produces no
measurable accuracy difference.

\textbf{Robustness to document-level clustering.} 406 items are not 406 independent draws: they trace
to 34 source documents (Section~\ref{sec:models}, Section~\ref{app:provenance}), and several
contradiction-detection items link two documents at once. We re-ran \emph{all 66} pairwise
comparisons under a document-clustered bootstrap---resampling the 27 connected components formed by
unioning documents that co-occur in any contradiction item, rather than resampling items
directly---not only the comparisons this paper singles out as findings, since checking only
hand-picked pairs would risk an unrepresentatively reassuring picture. The clustered test finds
\textbf{29 of 66} pairs significant at $p<0.05$ (item-level: 36) and \textbf{11} surviving Bonferroni
correction (item-level: 15--16, itself boundary-sensitive to Monte Carlo noise across independent
10,000-resample runs). \textbf{Nine pairs flip significance direction} under clustering---all among
comparisons this paper does not otherwise discuss. Every comparison this paper specifically reports
as a finding, including the human-reference comparison in Section~\ref{sec:human}, agrees in
direction between the two tests: the correction changes the overall significance count meaningfully
(36$\to$29) without overturning any conclusion this paper draws. Section~\ref{app:clustering}
reports the full 66-pair comparison and the clustering construction.
