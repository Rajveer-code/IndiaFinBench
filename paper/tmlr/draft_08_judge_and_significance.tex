\subsection{Cross-Model Judge}
\label{sec:judge}

An LLM-as-judge audit is only as convincing as the judge's independence from what it is judging.
The judge used in our initial pilot (Appendix~\ref{app:judge}) was Gemini 2.5 Flash---one of the
twelve evaluated models. To assess whether this affects the audit, we additionally judge every REG/NUM/TMP
prediction with \textbf{phi4-mini} (Microsoft Phi, 3.8B parameters), run locally via Ollama at zero
cost. Phi shares no model family with any of the twelve benchmarked systems. We call this a
\textbf{cross-model} judge, not an independent or ground-truth one: phi4-mini is a small model, and
disagreement between it and Gemini 2.5 Flash is compatible with either judge being wrong, which is
why we pair it with human adjudication rather than treating its verdict as final.

The cross-model pass covers \textbf{every} REG/NUM/TMP prediction---344 items $\times$ 12 models
$=$ 4{,}128 judgements---not only the items strict scoring marks wrong. Full coverage is what makes
a \emph{judge rejection rate of strict-correct predictions} estimable for the first time: the
original audit design, by construction, could only characterise strict-to-judge overturns. CON is excluded from both judges, matching
the pipeline's existing rationale (Section~\ref{sec:scoring}): it is scored by exact Yes/No match
against an unambiguous binary label, which does not benefit from semantic review. Excluding it is
conservative for the disagreement rates we report below, not convenient: CON is the one task where
strict and judge-based scoring would agree almost by construction, so its absence removes a source
of trivial agreement rather than manufacturing disagreement elsewhere.

Before committing to the full pass we validated the judge on two small, targeted samples: phi4-mini
agreed with all eight items Gemini 2.5 Flash itself answered correctly under strict scoring (a
sanity check, not evidence of discrimination), and on eight DeepSeek-R1-Distill items marked
incorrect under strict scoring, flipped seven to correct---each a genuine format mismatch---while
correctly holding the eighth, where the predicted figure simply did not match the reference. The
pilot showed that phi4-mini both overturned strict failures and retained cases where the substantive
answer remained incorrect.

Table~\ref{tab:judgereliability} reports, per model, the strict-to-judge overturn rate, the judge
rejection rate of strict-correct predictions (estimable for the first time here, since this pass
covers every item rather than only strict failures), and the resulting judge-augmented accuracy
(Appendix~\ref{app:regimetable} adds the judge-only figure). We avoid ``false negative''/``false
positive'': those terms presuppose the judge is ground truth, which it is not. On the 874 items
Gemini 2.5 Flash originally judged, phi4-mini agrees with it on 698 (\textbf{79.9\% raw agreement})---
neither suspiciously high (rubber-stamping) nor low enough to call either judge unreliable, but real,
bounded disagreement between two models neither of which we treat as ground truth. To resolve that
disagreement and check whether it tracks human judgment, we hand-adjudicate 238 items: the 174
REG/NUM/TMP items where Gemini and phi4-mini disagree, plus a stratified 64-item control sample where
they agree (Appendix~\ref{app:judge} gives the full sampling protocol). Adjudication is scored
against the judge verdicts as they stood when the sample was drawn, so every disagreement item
resolves to exactly one judge by construction; a later data-quality fix (Section~\ref{sec:models})
that corrected truncated reference text for a few items is reported independently rather than folded
in here. The adjudicator concurred with consensus on 89.1\% of control items; on the 174 disagreement
items, the human matched Gemini on 56.9\% and phi4-mini on 43.1\%, widest on numerical reasoning
(Appendix~\ref{app:judge} has the per-task breakdown). Neither judge is right often enough on these
disputed cases to be ground truth, and phi4-mini's lower share does not argue against using it as the
primary judge---that choice rests on full coverage, not on accuracy over Gemini.
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} returns to this as a limitation of the cross-model judge design.

\textbf{A pre-registered decision rule met an ambiguity it did not anticipate.} Before running the
full pass we committed to a rule: if the cross-model judge supports DeepSeek-R1-Distill's
strict-to-top reversal, report it as a central finding; if not, let discriminative coverage
(Section~\ref{sec:effsize}) carry more of the paper's weight instead. The rule presupposed a single
judge regime, but there are two: judge-augmented (Section~\ref{sec:regime}) satisfies it
exactly---DeepSeek ties for 1st---while judge-only, where the same judge's verdict is taken as final
rather than only ever overturning, does not---DeepSeek ranks 7th of twelve there, tied with
Qwen3-32B. We report both rather than choosing the reading the rule anticipated: the reversal is real
under judge-augmented scoring and is itself evidence for the paper's actual thesis, that which of two
defensible ways of applying a judge materially changes the answer. Discriminative coverage is a
coequal finding, not a fallback triggered by the gate's failure.

\textbf{What survives the judge audit.} Attributing every strict failure to a task-conditioned
category (domain-knowledge, numerical, temporal, or context-grounding; Appendix~\ref{sec:errortax-sub})
shows the reclassification is uneven across models, not uniform. DeepSeek-R1-Distill's 101 strict
failures across all four tasks (99 on REG/NUM/TMP, the figure behind the 93.9\% reclassification rate
in Section~\ref{sec:regime}, plus 2 on CON, which is never judge-reviewed) collapse to 8 residual---the
largest correction of any model and the basis for its judge-augmented reversal---while Gemma 3~4B,
despite a comparable 72.1\% reclassification rate,
keeps a residual error rate 1.7--3$\times$ every other profiled model's. Appendix~\ref{sec:errortax-sub}
reports the full per-model breakdown
and representative failure examples.

\subsection{Statistical Significance and Performance Tiers}
\label{sec:significance}

Paired bootstrap significance testing (100,000 resamples, verified stable across 5 independent
seeds) across all 66 model pairs finds 36 pairs significantly different at $p < 0.05$, of which
15--16 survive Bonferroni correction ($\alpha = 0.05/66 \approx 0.00076$): the count is 16 under 4
of 5 seeds and 15 under the fifth, because one pair (DeepSeek-R1-Distill vs.\ Llama 4 Scout 17B) sits
within Monte Carlo noise of the threshold itself ($p \approx 0.0007$--$0.0008$ across seeds). We
report the range rather than a single seed's count, since a point estimate here would understate how
close that one comparison sits to the correction boundary. Three descriptive performance clusters
organize the results under strict scoring; their boundaries are robust at the top and bottom of the
ranking but statistical, not categorical, in the middle.

\textbf{Tier 1 --- strong performers (81.5--89.7\%):} Gemini 2.5 Flash, Qwen3-32B, LLaMA-3.3-70B,
Llama 4 Scout 17B, and Kimi K2, all four of the latter statistically indistinguishable from one
another ($p$ = 0.06--0.79), forming a genuine plateau rather than a fine-grained ranking.
\textbf{The remaining seven models (70--79\%)} do not separate into a clean second and third tier:
Gemma 3 4B (78.8\%) is significantly below only the five Tier 1 models and is statistically
indistinguishable from every other model in the field ($p$ = 0.21--0.73), including
DeepSeek-R1-Distill, Mistral-7B, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and both GPT-OSS variants. Under strict scoring the
bottom seven models form one broad, largely undifferentiated cluster rather than the two-tier
structure a cursory read of the ranking would suggest.

Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to two comparisons inside the Tier 1 plateau---Llama 4 Scout
17B vs.\ LLaMA-3.3-70B and GPT-OSS 120B vs.\ 20B---where a large parameter difference produces no
measurable accuracy difference.

\textbf{Robustness to document-level clustering.} 406 items are not 406 independent draws: they trace
to 34 source documents (Section~\ref{sec:models}, Section~\ref{app:provenance}), and several
contradiction-detection items link two documents at once. We re-ran \emph{all 66} pairwise
comparisons under a document-clustered bootstrap---resampling the 27 connected components formed by
unioning documents that co-occur in any contradiction item, rather than resampling items
directly---not only the comparisons this paper singles out as findings, since checking only
hand-picked pairs would risk an unrepresentatively reassuring picture. The clustered test finds
\textbf{29 of 66} pairs significant at $p<0.05$ (item-level: 36) and \textbf{11} surviving Bonferroni
correction (item-level: 15--16, itself boundary-sensitive to Monte Carlo noise across independent
10,000-resample runs). \textbf{Nine pairs flip significance direction} under clustering---all among
comparisons this paper does not otherwise discuss. Every comparison this paper specifically reports
as a finding, including the human-reference comparison in Section~\ref{sec:human}, agrees in
direction between the two tests: the correction changes the overall significance count meaningfully
(36$\to$29) without overturning any conclusion this paper draws. Section~\ref{app:clustering}
reports the full 66-pair comparison and the clustering construction.
